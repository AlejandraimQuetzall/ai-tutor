# Encoding

You can look straight at a page and still have nothing to say about it five minutes later. Seeing the words is not the same thing as keeping them. Encoding is the process of turning an experience into a memory trace. Attention decides what enters that trace. If you do not attend to a page, that page has little chance of being encoded.

Picture a slide that stays up while you answer a message. Your eyes passed over the definition. The words were on the screen, and they were in the room with you. None of that builds a trace. Attention is the gate in front of encoding. A paragraph you did not select does not wait around to be repaired by a later stage. Sleep, review, and a tidy notebook cannot store a page that never became a trace.

Getting in is only the first half. A trace can exist and still be hard to find when you need it. Elaborative encoding connects the new idea to something you already know, which makes the trace easier to find later. A definition copied with no link to prior knowledge is weaker than the same definition tied to an example you can picture.

Suppose you have just met the word "consolidation." Copying the glossary line into a notebook feels like work, because the line is now in your handwriting. A stronger encoding attaches the word to a night you already remember: you studied one chapter, slept, and the next morning the argument was easier to retell than it had been at midnight. The new word now has a place to hang. The strength is in that link. A cleaner sentence, or a sentence that sounds more official, does not replace it. If you cannot point at the older idea, you have a quotation, not an encoding.

Repetition is the strategy most people reach for, and the feeling it creates is easy to trust too soon. Repeating a word without meaning it is a weak form of encoding. The sound can feel familiar while the idea still has no durable trace. Meaning, not the number of repetitions, is what gives encoding its strength.

Say a new term ten times and it starts to sound like a word you have met. That familiarity is real. It is also a poor guide. Familiarity can tell you that the sound has been around. It does not tell you that you can explain the idea, use it on a fresh example, or answer a question that does not already contain the term. One explanation that ties the idea to a scene you can picture does more for the trace than another lap through the same sentence.

Before you leave a paragraph, do one check. Name the claim in your own words, then name the older idea it hangs on. If you cannot name either, you have visited the paragraph. You have not encoded it yet.
